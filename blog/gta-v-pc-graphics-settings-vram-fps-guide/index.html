GTA V PC Graphics Settings: FPS, VRAM and Image Quality

Published November 11, 2026 · By Digital Racer

GTA V on PC can run on a wide range of hardware, but a preset name does not explain which component is limiting performance. Use a repeatable scene and adjust the costly setting rather than changing everything simultaneously.

Start with resolution and a fixed comparison scene

Record the screen resolution, refresh rate, display mode and frame-rate limit before adjusting graphics. Test in a repeatable location at similar time of day and weather where possible. Different areas of the map can vary in traffic, shadows and scene complexity, so a comparison between a quiet road and a crowded city is misleading. Use a trustworthy frame-time overlay or in-game benchmark if available in your installed edition. Observe average performance and noticeable spikes rather than treating a brief maximum FPS as the only result.

  • Keep the same scene, camera and gameplay conditions between tests.
  • Save a screenshot of the original settings menu.

Understand what VRAM usage indicates

Video memory stores textures and other graphics resources needed by the GPU. If a game exceeds practical available VRAM or streams resources aggressively, you may see loading or frame-time problems. But a high reported VRAM allocation is not automatically a failure, and more VRAM does not guarantee higher FPS in every scene. Read the actual edition's graphics memory indicator and compare it with observed behavior. Texture settings often affect memory requirements, while lighting and shadow changes can have different compute costs.

  • Do not reduce every texture setting merely because memory allocation looks high.
  • Separate VRAM pressure from system RAM and disk bottlenecks.

Test shadows, reflections and anti-aliasing

Shadow quality, reflection detail, post-processing and anti-aliasing can change GPU workload in different ways. Start by identifying a setting that visibly affects the scene you care about. Lower one expensive effect, rerun the test and note whether frame-time stability improves. An anti-aliasing method may smooth edges at the cost of image clarity or performance; compare stationary detail and moving objects. Some advanced-distance settings can increase load with large view ranges. Avoid assuming every item labeled ultra delivers a meaningful visual benefit at your display size.

  • Change one high-cost option at a time.
  • Keep a brief note of both the visual difference and the measured response.

Detect CPU limitations before lowering everything

If the GPU is not consistently busy but frame times are poor, consider CPU-side factors such as dense simulation, background tasks or single-thread bottlenecks. A modern graphics card may still be constrained by an older processor or crowded background environment. Check system RAM pressure, storage activity and temperatures, and compare results in a less demanding scene. Lowering resolution primarily reduces GPU work; it may not fix CPU-side stutter. Do not buy hardware based solely on a low GPU percentage without investigating the actual workload.

  • Check per-core CPU behavior instead of only total utilization.
  • Test with optional overlays and background recorders closed.

Build two usable profiles rather than one perfect preset

Consider a quality profile for relaxed exploration and a smoother profile for more responsive play. Keep the differences understandable: resolution scale, shadow quality, reflections or a small set of costly effects. A stable frame-rate cap can sometimes improve pacing when uncapped performance fluctuates, but the best choice depends on your display and hardware. Recheck after major driver or game updates because optimizations may change. There is no need to preserve a visually imperceptible effect if it causes repeated stutters on your own machine.

  • Choose a setting you can compare in normal play, not only a benchmark.
  • Document the final stable preset so it is easy to restore.
